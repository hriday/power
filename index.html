How much power is that, really?

Headlines throw around megawatts and gigawatts like everyone knows what they mean. Most of us don't, and that's fine. Here you can build up a feel for it, from a single light bulb to a whole city, and then play with a data centre to see how it really compares.

    Step 5

    Power vs energy: speed vs distance

    This is the #1 source of confusion. Power (watts) is like your car's speed. Energy (watt-hours) is like the distance you've driven. Your power bill charges you for energy, in kilowatt-hours (kWh).

    🚗

    Speed × time = distance

    100 km/h for 2 hours = 200 km

    is just like
    ⚡

    Power × time = energy

    1 kW for 2 hours = 2 kWh

    Step 6

    Headline decoder

    Six questions to ask the next time you read a big number about power or water.

    1 Is it MW or MWh?

    MW is a rate (how fast). MWh is an amount (how much). "Uses 500 MWh" means nothing until you know the time: was that a day? A year? A data centre drawing 100 MW uses 2,400 MWh every day.

    2 Is that the maximum, or what really flows?

    Power plants are labelled with their maximum output. A 3.5 MW wind turbine averages about 1.2 MW because the wind isn't always blowing. A 100 MW solar farm in Alberta averages under 20 MW because of night and clouds. Nuclear runs close to its maximum almost all the time.

    3 "Enough to power X homes": which homes, and when?

    Most of these claims assume an average home uses about 1 kW. That's a fair yardstick, but on a cold January evening the same home might draw 5 kW or more. Use it to get a feel for scale, not as an exact number.

    4 Proposed, approved or actually running?

    Alberta's grid operator has received data centre requests adding up to more than the whole province's peak demand (about 12 GW), but has said only around 1.2 GW can be connected in the near term. A 7.5 GW proposal is a plan, not a building.

    5 Tiny × huge = ?

    One AI chatbot question uses roughly 0.3 Wh, about like running an LED bulb for two minutes (estimates vary). That's tiny. But a billion questions a day averages out to about 12 MW, a small town's worth. Both halves of that are true.

    6 Water: borrowed or used up? Counted where?

    Withdrawn water is borrowed and mostly returned. Consumed water evaporates and is gone from the local supply. Also check whether the number includes water used at the power plants, which is often several times bigger than the data centre's own use. That's why one AI question is reported as anywhere from about 5 drops (Google's 0.26 mL, data centre only) to a few spoonfuls (estimates that include power plants).

    Cheat sheet

    Glossary

    Watt (W)
    The basic unit of power: how fast electricity is being used or made.
    Kilowatt (kW)
    1,000 watts. A kettle uses about 1.5 kW while it's boiling.
    Megawatt (MW)
    1,000 kW, or a million watts. Roughly 1,000 average homes.
    Gigawatt (GW)
    1,000 MW, or a billion watts. About one large nuclear reactor, or all of Calgary.
    Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
    Energy: 1 kW used for 1 hour. This is what your power bill counts.
    Capacity
    The most a power plant can produce. What it actually produces on average is usually less.
    Capacity factor
    Average output ÷ maximum output. Nuclear is about 90%, wind about 35% and solar about 15–20%.
    Peak demand
    The highest power a grid needs at one moment, often on the coldest or hottest evening of the year.
    PUE
    Power Usage Effectiveness: a data centre's total power ÷ the power its computers use. Lower is better, and 1.0 would be perfect.
    WUE
    Water Usage Effectiveness: litres of water a data centre uses up for each kWh its computers use. Evaporative cooling is about 1.5–2 L, and closed-loop cooling is close to 0.
    Withdrawn vs consumed water
    Withdrawn water is taken and mostly returned (often warmer). Consumed water evaporates and is lost to the local supply.
    GPU
    Graphics Processing Unit: the type of chip that runs most modern AI.
    Where do these numbers come from?

    Everything here is rounded so it's easy to remember. Different sources will give somewhat different numbers, so treat them as "about right" rather than exact.

    • City demand: estimated from annual electricity delivered by local utilities, divided by 8,760 hours in a year. Red Deer ≈ 90 MW, Calgary ≈ 1.1 GW and Toronto ≈ 2.9 GW on average. Peaks are higher.
    • Greater Tokyo: the Tokyo power company's service area (Kanto region, about 45 million people) uses roughly 260 TWh a year, ≈ 30 GW on average, with summer peaks around 55 GW. The 37 million figure is the metro population.
    • Daily curve: an illustrative winter weekday shape (low overnight, evening peak), not measured data for any specific day.
    • Average home: ≈ 1 kW (about 8,800 kWh a year). Alberta homes use somewhat less and homes with electric heat use more.
    • AI chips: "power per chip" includes the chip's share of its server (memory, networking, CPUs). An H100-class chip is ≈ 1.3 kW all-in and a Blackwell-class chip is ≈ 1.9 kW.
    • Wind turbine: 3.5 MW maximum at a ~35% capacity factor, so ≈ 1.2 MW average. Nuclear reactor: ≈ 1 GW.
    • Data centre water: evaporative cooling ≈ 1.8 L per kWh of computer energy (operators report about 1.1–2.5). Hybrid or outside-air cooling ≈ 0.4 L (an industry target for new sites). Closed-loop or dry cooling ≈ 0.05 L, but it costs more electricity.
    • Power-plant water: ≈ 4.5 L consumed per kWh, a recent US-grid average. It varies hugely: wind and solar use almost none, and thermal and hydro plants use much more.
    • Water yardsticks: an Olympic pool ≈ 2.5 million L. Canadians use ≈ 223 L per person per day at home (Statistics Canada, 2021). Calgary uses ≈ 600 million L a day.
    • Per AI question: Google reports 0.26 mL of water and 0.24 Wh for a median Gemini text prompt (2025, on-site water only).
    • Grid totals: Alberta's record peak is ≈ 12 GW. Canada averages ≈ 65 GW, the US ≈ 470 GW and the world ≈ 3.4 TW of electricity.
